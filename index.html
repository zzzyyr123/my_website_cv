About

I am a Postdoctoral Researcher in the Department of Environmental Health and Engineering at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health, Johns Hopkins University. I received my Ph.D. in Genetics from Fudan University, with joint doctoral research training at the Shanghai Institute of Nutrition and Health, Chinese Academy of Sciences.

My research interests span environmental and lifestyle exposomics, human phenomics, and systems-level multi-omics analysis. I am interested in combining population-based studies and real-world health information with molecular measurements to characterize exposure-related biological variation and investigate pathways associated with health and disease.

Research at Johns Hopkins

At Johns Hopkins, my research interests center on integrating population-level health information with molecular data to better understand how environmental and lifestyle exposures are related to human health. I am interested in working with exposomics resources such as HHEAR and exploring the integration of multi-omics profiles with health information, including electronic health record-derived phenotypes.

A key part of this work is developing robust and reproducible analytical approaches for harmonizing heterogeneous datasets, integrating complementary molecular layers, and identifying interpretable patterns that can inform environmental and population health research.

Selected Research Projects

Lifestyle Exposures, Peripheral Immunity, and Chronic Health

Ph.D. research

Integrated 183 exposures, 1,332 immunophenotypes, whole-blood transcriptomics, and plasma metabolomics from 1,001 participants to investigate exposure-related immune variation. The study identified 81 significant signals, with sleep and diet emerging as prominent exposure domains associated with immune cells and function. Further analyses examined links among exposures, immune phenotypes, transcriptomic and metabolomic intermediates, and health outcomes. Exposome-omics indices were developed and evaluated in eight external cohorts.

Dietary Inflammation, Gut Microbiota, Metabolites, and Insulin Resistance

Ph.D. research

Integrated gut metagenomic and plasma metabolomic data from 682 participants to investigate potential microbial and metabolic links between the Dietary Inflammatory Score and insulin resistance. The analyses identified five microbial species associated with both dietary inflammation and insulin resistance, including mediation signals involving Eubacterium eligens and Streptococcus salivarius. Metabolomic and pathway analyses highlighted triglycerides, branched-chain amino acids, HDL, and pectin degradation-related processes.
